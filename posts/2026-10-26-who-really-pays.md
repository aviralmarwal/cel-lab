Swarajpuri Road is one of the best addresses in Gaya. Rampur Harijan Toli is one of the cheapest. Land on the first is worth many times land on the second, and that difference is written into the government's own market value register. Yet when it comes to vacant land tax, the plot on Rampur Harijan Toli pays an effective rate on its value of 0.094 percent, and the plot on Swarajpuri Road pays 0.021 percent. The poorer land is taxed, in proportion to what it is worth, more than four times as heavily as the richer.

Nobody in Gaya designed it that way. Bihar's property tax rules charge vacant land a flat amount by the width of the road it sits on: ₹5 per square metre on a principal main road, ₹4 on a main road, ₹3 on anything else. A plot on an ordinary road pays ₹3 whether the land under it is worth ₹5,000 or ₹50,000 a square metre. Divide a fixed charge by a falling value and the effective rate rises as you move outward. The periphery pays more because the tax never asked what the land was worth.

## Every city has a gradient. Not every tax does.

Land values in Indian cities fall with distance from the centre, usually steeply. In Begusarai the government's own valuation register puts core land at ₹17,297 per square metre and land near the airport at ₹5,930, a ratio of 2.2 to 1. In Ambala the collector rate runs from ₹27,500 per square yard in the planned sectors to ₹7,200 in the bastis on the edge. In Ludhiana the spread between GT Road and the cheapest residential colony is 46 to 1.

The question is how much of that gradient the tax picks up. We measured it for every city in our database as the ratio between the tax on a standard plot in a typical core locality and the tax on the same plot in a typical peripheral locality, using the median of several localities on each side so that one unusual street does not drive the result.

The answer ranges from more than nine to less than one.

At the top are cities where the tax is tied directly to a government land valuation. Amritsar, Delhi, Kolhapur, Warangal, Vijayawada, Visakhapatnam and Hyderabad all have gradients above four. In these cities a plot at the centre pays, per square metre, four to nine times what a plot at the edge pays, which is roughly what you would expect if the tax were tracking value.

In the middle are the cities that set their own zone or unit-area rates. Delhi is the exception at the top of the chart, and gets there through a deliberately designed table of categories; most of the others, including Ahmedabad, Lucknow, Surat, Vadodara and Indore, land between 1.5 and 1.7. The zones are real but coarse. Gwalior is the clearest case: its Zone 1 and Zone 2 rates differ by about 12 percent, while market land values between the same two zones differ by something like 15 times.

At the bottom are the cities where the tax has no gradient at all. Coimbatore's calculator returns the identical figure for every ward since a 2015 reform, ₹1,584 a year on our standard plot in Race Course and in Saravanampatti alike. Dewas, once you hold the zoning constant, is flat. In Ranchi the most prestigious ward in the city and a peripheral one pay the same rate whenever they share a road-width tier, which we confirmed by checking two wards against each other on the municipal portal. And then there is Kalaburagi, where the periphery median actually exceeds the core, driven by a single outer road whose guidance value is more than double every nominally central locality we tested.

## A gradient can be created overnight

Punjab shows what happens when a state changes the design. Before 2013, Ludhiana and Jalandhar charged every vacant plot the same amount regardless of where it was. In 2011 every locality in Jalandhar paid an identical ₹333.33. The tax had no spatial content whatsoever.

The 2013-14 reform tied the tax to the collector rate, the state's official land valuation. The effect in Ludhiana was immediate and extreme: GT Road, in the commercial heart of the city, now pays about ₹2,886 a year on a standard plot against ₹62 for Inder Vihar and Laxmi Colony on the edge, a 46-fold difference that is the steepest confirmed anywhere in our database. Jalandhar, under the very same reform, ended up with a gradient of about nine between its most and least expensive localities but only around 1.1 between the median core and median peripheral locality, because its collector rates happen to be much flatter across the city than Ludhiana's.

Same law, same year, two very different outcomes. The design decides whether the tax has a gradient; the local land market decides how steep it is.

## Why the gradient is the whole point

If the only purpose of a vacant land tax were revenue, none of this would matter much. The amounts are small, and a city could collect the same total from a flat charge as from a graded one.

But the reason economists have argued for taxing idle land since Henry George is not revenue. It is the effect on where and when people build. A plot at the centre of a city, where land is expensive and services already exist, is the plot the city most wants developed. A charge that is proportional to value bears down hardest on exactly that plot, and makes holding it empty expensive relative to holding a plot at the edge. Development is nudged inward. A flat charge does the opposite: it is trivial at the centre, where it is a rounding error on a valuable asset, and heaviest at the edge, where it is a real cost on cheap land that the city would rather not see built on yet.

Fifteen of the 36 cities in our database with usable data, over 40 percent, have a gradient below 1.7. For those cities the vacant land tax, whatever it collects, is not doing the job it is named for. In Coimbatore, Ranchi, Dewas and the Bihar cities it is doing nothing at all, or pushing the wrong way.

This is the second half of the natural experiment we described in the first post of this series. Some cities have tax designs that penalise idle land at the centre. Others have designs that are indifferent to location, or that lean on the periphery. If holding costs shape urban form, these two groups of cities should have been spreading at different rates for the past two decades. That is the comparison we are now building.
