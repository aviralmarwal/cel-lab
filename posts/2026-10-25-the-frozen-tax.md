In 2022 the Fifth Municipal Valuation Committee in Delhi did what such committees are set up to do. It looked at the unit area values on which the city's property tax is based, found that they had not been touched since the system was introduced in 2004, and recommended raising them by 37 percent. The recommendation was not adopted. The municipal corporation's online calculator still uses the 2004 numbers today.

This means that the owner of an empty plot in Vasant Vihar pays exactly the same rupee amount for leaving it empty as the owner did in 2015, in 2010, and in 2005. Consumer prices in India have more than doubled since 2011 alone. In real terms, the tax has been quietly cut by more than half, without a single council vote.

Delhi is not unusual. When we built our database of vacant land tax across 38 Indian cities, we expected to find rates that went up and down with politics, elections and budget cycles. What we found instead, in city after city, was a flat line.

## How long is long

Some of the freezes we found are hard to believe until you see the paperwork.

**Chennai's Basic Street Rate**, the location-linked component of its property tax, was frozen for roughly 24 years, from about 1998 until March 2022. We did not have to infer this: the Madras High Court recorded it in the course of litigation over the 2022 revision.

**Bangalore's unit area values** for vacant land were unchanged from 2000 until 2016, a point the corporation's own FAQ confirms. After the 2016 revision they were frozen again, and remain so as we write. Two freezes, 25 years, one revision.

**Patna** taxes vacant land using a factor set by the width of the road. The factor has not been revised for the road tiers we studied since at least 1993, and the classification of roads itself was left untouched for over 30 years. A plot on Boring Road pays about ₹550 a year today, as it did when the plot's current owners were children.

**Gaya** sits under the same Bihar law, which requires the rate to be revised at least every five years. The city's live tax calculator returns the identical number for every year from 2015-16 through 2026-27.

**Lucknow** has had no confirmed revision of its ward-wise rate schedule since 2010. **Mangalore's** guidance values were unchanged for at least fourteen years under its old regime. In **Nizamabad**, actual tax records show plots whose assessed value was fixed on the day they were first assessed and never looked at again, so one plot assessed in 2008 and an identical one assessed in 2023 can be paying wildly different amounts for the same land.

None of these cities decided to cut the vacant land tax. Each one simply did not raise it, year after year, until inflation had done the cutting for them.

## Why cities let it happen

The pattern is easy to explain once you look at who pays and who decides.

A revision is a visible act. It appears in the newspapers, it draws deputations to the mayor, and in a city like Delhi it can reach the courts. Leaving the rate alone is invisible. No bill goes up, no one protests, and the loss of real revenue is spread so thinly across the years that it never becomes a story.

Vacant land is also a particularly easy tax to neglect, because it has a small and well-connected constituency. Most residents do not own an empty plot. The people who do tend to own several, and tend to know the councillor.

And in many states the tax is not formally linked to anything that moves. Hyderabad, where the tax is simply half a percent of the registration department's circle rate, has no freeze problem: when circle rates rise, the tax follows automatically, and the council never has to vote for an increase. Delhi, Chennai, Bangalore, Lucknow and the Bihar cities all set their own rate tables, and a table that someone has to decide to change is a table that does not change.

> A table that someone has to decide to change is a table that does not change.

## Freeze, then shock

The frozen tax does not stay frozen forever. What happens instead is that the pressure builds until a court, a state government or a new assessment system forces the issue, and then the rate moves all at once.

Chennai's 24-year freeze ended in April 2022 with a revised rate table and a new flat levy of 12.4 percent. On our standard plot in a core locality, the annual tax went from about ₹3,400 to about ₹7,100, a little more than double, in a single year.

Indore kept its per-square-foot rates flat for roughly a decade and then, in March 2023, passed a single council resolution reassigning 531 colonies to new rate zones. A.B. Road jumped from Zone 3 to Zone 1, skipping Zone 2 entirely. Residents reported bill increases of between 12 and 63 percent, and the new rate table alone is enough to explain the whole of that range.

Kolkata's case is the largest in our database. When the city replaced its old annual rental value system with unit area assessment in 2021, it also set the multiplier for vacant land at twice that for a built house. The tax on a plot in Alipore went from roughly ₹9,500 a year to roughly ₹36,400, an increase of 282 percent.

In each case the shock was the accumulated arrears of a decade or more of doing nothing, collected in one bill. It is not surprising that these revisions were unpopular. It would be surprising if they were not.

## What this means for the question we care about

Our interest in this tax is what it does to the shape of cities. The theory says that a meaningful charge on idle land encourages owners to build or sell rather than wait, and so pulls development inward instead of letting it leapfrog to the edge.

A frozen rate makes that mechanism weaker every year. If the tax was a nuisance in 2004 and a rounding error by 2025, then whatever effect it once had on holding decisions has been fading for two decades, and the cities that froze their rates have been running an unplanned experiment in what happens when you remove it. The cities that index to market value, mostly in the south, have been running the opposite experiment. That contrast is what the next stage of our work will try to use.

The policy lesson is older than our database and simpler than any regression. A tax that has to be revised by a vote will not be revised. If a city wants a vacant land tax that still exists in ten years, it has to be tied, in law, to something that moves on its own.
