Take a residential plot of 1,200 square feet. Leave it empty. Now ask what the city charges you each year for the privilege of doing nothing with it.

In Mumbai, in a typical core locality, the answer is a little over ₹82,000. On Malabar Hill it crosses ₹1.5 lakh. In Hyderabad or Vijayawada it is somewhere between ₹37,000 and ₹49,000. In Delhi it is about ₹3,800. In Bangalore, ₹1,500. In Patna, ₹550. In Vesu, the most expensive neighbourhood in Surat, it is ₹201. And in Bareilly it is zero, because the Nagar Nigam does not tax vacant land at all.

That is a spread of several hundred times for the same thing in the same country. Land prices do not explain it. Land in Vesu is expensive by any standard except south Mumbai's, and the gap between the two is nowhere near four hundred times. Most of the difference is design.

## Where the numbers come from

Over the past year we have been putting together a database of vacant land tax across Indian cities, going back where we can to 2001. It now covers 38 cities in 16 states, from Mumbai and Delhi down to Begusarai and Dewas. For each city we worked out, from the municipal gazette, the official tax calculator, the registration department's circle rates or, in a few cases, an actual tax receipt from a resident, what an owner pays on a standard plot in a dozen or so localities running from the centre of the city to its edge.

The figures above are the median of the core localities in each city for 2025, expressed per square metre so that cities using different reference plots can sit on one chart. Where we could not confirm a number we left the cell blank rather than guess, which is why Varanasi, whose 2025 rate we have not yet been able to verify, is missing from the chart.

## Four ways to tax an empty plot

Reading through 38 municipal rulebooks, you find that Indian cities have settled on four quite different answers to the question of what a vacant plot should pay.

The first group ties the tax to the market. Hyderabad charges half a percent of the land's value as recorded by the registration department, so when the circle rate doubles, the tax doubles with it. Mumbai, the Andhra and Telangana cities, the Karnataka cities and, after reforms in 2013, the Punjab cities all work this way, with various factors and discounts applied. These cities sit at the top of the chart.

The second group sets its own rate table. The city divides itself into zones or categories and fixes a rate per square foot for each, with no formal link to what land actually sells for. Delhi, Chennai, Bangalore, Kolkata, Lucknow, Indore and the Gujarat cities belong here. The rates can be sensible when they are set, but there is nothing that forces the city to update them, and most do not. Delhi's unit area values have not been revised since 2004. Chennai's street rates were frozen for over two decades until a court case and a 2022 reform. We will come back to the frozen cities in a later post, because they deserve one of their own.

The third group charges a flat amount by the width of the road the plot sits on. This is the Bihar model, written into the state's rules: ₹5 per square metre on a principal main road, ₹4 on a main road, ₹3 elsewhere. Patna, Gaya and Begusarai all use it, and Ranchi has a version of it. A plot on a prime road in Patna and a plot on an equally wide road at the city's edge pay the same, whatever either is worth.

The fourth group, so far consisting of Bareilly, simply exempts vacant land.

## Same law, three different answers

The clearest illustration of how much design matters comes from Gujarat, where three large cities tax property under the same state act, with the same formula: a base rate, multiplied by a location factor, multiplied by a factor for the type of property.

Ahmedabad applies no discount for vacant plots. The open-plot factor is 1.0, and a plot in Vastrapur pays about ₹7,000 a year. Vadodara applies a 50 percent discount, and a comparable plot in Sayajigunj pays about ₹1,100. Surat applies a 90 percent discount, and Vesu pays ₹201. Three councils, one law, a 35-fold difference in what it costs to hold land idle.

Kolkata went the other way. Under its unit area system, standalone vacant land carries a usage multiplier of 2.0, double the multiplier for a built house on the same plot. It is the only city in our database that deliberately taxes an empty plot more heavily than an occupied one. When the system came in, the tax on a plot in Alipore went from about ₹9,500 a year to about ₹36,000.

## Why we care

We are not collecting these numbers to rank cities by how much they charge. The question behind the database is whether the cost of holding land idle has any effect on the shape a city takes.

The argument is old and simple. If an empty plot costs almost nothing to keep, an owner can wait for prices to rise and build later, or never. Development skips over the vacant plot to cheaper land further out, and the city spreads. If the plot costs real money to hold, the owner has a reason to build or sell, and growth fills in rather than spreading out. Whether this happens in practice, and how long it takes, is what we are now trying to measure, using the density of each city at different distances from its centre over the same twenty-five years the tax data covers.

What the chart already tells us is that India is running a natural experiment on an enormous scale. Thirty-eight cities, four designs, a range of several hundred times in the holding cost of land. If the tax matters for sprawl, it should show up here.

*A note on the data. All figures are for a standard residential plot, confirmed from primary sources or extended from a confirmed adjacent year on a stated basis. Rajasthan and Haryana cities use a 350 square yard plot because smaller plots are exempt there; Gwalior uses 1,501 square feet for the same reason. Annual rupee figures quoted in the text are for a 1,200 square foot plot. The full methodology and the city-by-city sourcing notes will be released with the paper.*
